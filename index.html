01 · Source Material

The Theory — In Plain English

Before we build the graph, we need a story to work from. Here is the JFK conspiracy theory as documented in the source — in straightforward language.

Source: McKnight (2005) + HSCA (1979) Event: November 22, 1963 Conspiracy Theory

On November 22, 1963, President John F. Kennedy was shot and killed while riding in a motorcade through Dealey Plaza in Dallas, Texas. The Warren Commission, set up by President Johnson to investigate, concluded in 1964 that Lee Harvey Oswald acted alone — firing three shots from the sixth floor of the Texas School Book Depository.

But critics of that conclusion — including historian Gerald McKnight and the later House Select Committee on Assassinations (HSCA) in 1979 — argued that the evidence pointed elsewhere. The HSCA concluded there was "probably a conspiracy", finding acoustic evidence of a second gunman firing from the Grassy Knoll in front of the motorcade.

The theory points to several suspicious actors. Jack Ruby, a Dallas nightclub owner with known ties to organized crime, shot and killed Oswald just two days after the assassination — silencing the only suspect before he could talk. Oswald himself had connections to both the CIA and to pro-Cuba groups, and had defected to the Soviet Union before returning to the US.

The Warren Commission is accused of suppressing evidence. Key witnesses described hearing shots from multiple directions. The "magic bullet theory" — which required a single bullet to change direction multiple times to cause seven wounds — was widely ridiculed. J. Edgar Hoover (FBI Director) and the CIA both withheld information from the Commission, according to later investigations.

McKnight's research argues that the Commission knew its "lone gunman" conclusion was flawed but chose political stability over truth. The CIA, the Mafia, anti-Castro Cuban exiles, and even factions within the US military had documented motives to want Kennedy dead — he had rejected the Bay of Pigs invasion plan, was negotiating with Cuba, and was considering withdrawing from Vietnam.

The declassified JFK Records (2017–2023) revealed that the CIA had withheld files on Oswald for over 50 years, and that surveillance of Oswald in Mexico City weeks before the shooting had been deliberately obscured. The graph built from this network of actors and events tells a story very different from the official one.

03 · Methodology

The NLP Pipeline We Used

Four steps to go from a pile of documents to a story-telling knowledge graph.

01
Find the Entities
Named Entity Recognition (NER)
Read the source documents. Tag every person, organization, place, and event mentioned. Give each one a type label.
"Oswald" → Person
"Warren Commission" → Org
"Dealey Plaza" → Location
02
Find the Links
Relation Extraction (RE)
Look at how entities are described near each other in sentences. Extract typed relationship triples: (Subject → Verb → Object).
"Ruby shot Oswald"
→ Ruby – shot – Oswald
03

Temporal Event Sequencing

Nov 24: Ruby kills Oswald
04
Read the Story
Graph Traversal + Narrative

06 · Graph Narrative

The Story the Graph Tells

By following the edges from central nodes, a 3-act narrative emerges automatically — without reading every document.

ACT 1
The Network of Motives
The graph shows that at least four separate groups had documented reasons to want JFK dead — the CIA (fired after Bay of Pigs), the Mafia (targeted by RFK), Anti-Castro Cubans (abandoned at Bay of Pigs), and elements of the military (opposed to Vietnam withdrawal). Oswald sits at the centre of several of these networks simultaneously — a node with unusually high betweenness connecting the CIA, the Soviet Union, Cuba, and the official narrative.
Graph insight: Oswald is not the end node — he is the bridge node. Real perpetrators in a conspiracy are usually found behind the highest-betweenness person, not at them.
ACT 2
The Rupture and the Silence
The assassination itself (Nov 22) and Oswald's killing (Nov 24) are the two pivotal event nodes. The graph reveals a suspicious pattern: every path from Oswald to a potential co-conspirator was severed within 48 hours. Ruby's killing of Oswald cut the only thread that could have unraveled the network. The speed of this silencing, and Ruby's Mafia connections, make the "lone gunman" conclusion structurally implausible in the graph.
Graph insight: When the highest-betweenness node (Oswald) is removed from the graph within 48 hours of the event node, it is consistent with deliberate network pruning — eliminating the path from crime to perpetrator.
ACT 3
The Cover-Up Network
The Warren Commission outcome node connects backwards to LBJ (who formed it), the CIA and FBI (who withheld evidence from it), and Allen Dulles — the CIA Director JFK fired, who then served on the Commission. The HSCA (1979) created a competing outcome node: "probable conspiracy." The JFK Records Act (1992–2023) confirmed the CIA's sustained concealment. The cover-up, if it exists, was not a single act — it was a network of institutional choices.
Graph insight: The Warren Commission outcome node is connected to four entities who each had independent reasons to want a "lone gunman" conclusion — making institutional conspiracy more structurally stable than individual conspiracy.
07 · Our Findings

What the Graph Concludes

These are the specific findings from our knowledge graph analysis — derived from graph structure, not just from reading the text.

01
Oswald is a bridge, not an endpoint
In the graph, Oswald connects to the CIA, the Soviet Union, pro- and anti-Castro groups, and the official investigation. This is not the profile of a lone actor — it is the profile of a high-betweenness intermediary, typically found in the middle of a larger network.
02
The investigation was structurally compromised
The Warren Commission graph node has edges from LBJ (who formed it), CIA (who withheld files), FBI (who withheld evidence), and Allen Dulles (who was fired by JFK). A body is structurally incapable of independence when its connections include people with a direct stake in the outcome.
03
The network pruning pattern is suspicious
Ruby's killing of Oswald 48 hours after the assassination removed the only node that connected the crime to any potential co-conspirators. In graph theory terms, this is a targeted attack on the graph's most critical bridge — consistent with deliberate evidence elimination.
04
Four independent motive clusters existed
The graph shows CIA, Mafia, Anti-Castro Cubans, and military-industrial interests as four separate clusters — each with independent, documented motives. The "lone gunman" theory requires all of these to be coincidental. The conspiracy reading requires only one to have acted.
05
50 years of file concealment is a fact, not a theory
The CIA withheld Oswald files from 1963 to 2017–2023. This is not a conspiracy theory — it is a documented institutional fact confirmed by the JFK Records Act releases. Why an innocent surveillance relationship required 50 years of secrecy is an unanswered question.
06
Two official investigations reach opposite conclusions
The Warren Commission (1964) says lone gunman. The HSCA (1979) says probable conspiracy. Both are US government investigations. The fact that these two conclusions co-exist with no formal resolution means the JFK case remains officially unsettled — not merely theoretically disputed.

Our Conclusion

The knowledge graph does not prove who killed JFK. No analysis can do that without conclusive physical evidence. But the graph does reveal something important: the structural features of the network — Oswald as bridge node, the Warren Commission's compromised connections, the rapid removal of the key witness, and four independent motive clusters — are all inconsistent with a simple lone gunman event.

The graph supports the HSCA's 1979 conclusion of "probable conspiracy" — not as a definitive answer, but as the structurally more coherent reading of the available evidence. What NLP adds here is the ability to see the pattern of connections, not just the events — and patterns, in this case, speak loudly.

"The Warren Commission's conclusion was not wrong because of what it said — it was wrong because of what it refused to see." — Gerald McKnight, Breach of Trust (2005)
08 · Business Applications

Why This Matters for Business Analytics

The same knowledge graph pipeline applies directly to business intelligence and risk analysis contexts.

Fraud Detection
Map transactions, accounts, and individuals as nodes. High-betweenness accounts that connect otherwise unrelated clusters are likely hubs of fraudulent activity — the "Oswald" of the network.
Competitive Intelligence
Extract entities from competitor press releases and filings. Follow relationship edges to find which partnerships, investments, and personnel moves signal strategic pivots before they become public knowledge.
Supply Chain Risk
Model your supplier network as a graph. Nodes with the most connections are single points of failure — if removed, large parts of the chain break. Visualizing this in advance prevents the "48-hour crisis" pattern.
Regulatory Risk
Build graphs from enforcement actions, court cases, and lobbying records. When your firm shares multiple edges with entities under investigation, the graph shows exposure before any formal action begins.
M&A Due Diligence
Map the target company's relationships — investors, board members, past litigation, key customers. The graph reveals hidden liabilities that would take months to find through traditional document review.
Corporate Governance
Analyze board interlocks and executive networks as a graph. When the same individuals appear across multiple nodes in a dispute (like Allen Dulles on the Warren Commission), it signals structural conflict of interest.
